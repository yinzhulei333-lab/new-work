# Process

## Where the idea came from

I came to this course after four years of industrial design. My portfolio is full of objects — chairs, lamps, plastic casings — that I could make look good, but that never responded back. The question I kept circling was whether design could go beyond shaping a thing, and instead shape the relationship between a person and a thing. That question became the essay.

## How I worked

I did not write the README in one sitting. I drafted it over two days so the thinking could settle between sessions. The first pass was about objects being "too quiet" — the contrast between a tool you use and something you live with. The second pass brought in WindSinger as a concrete example, because the essay needed to land on something real rather than stay abstract. Each pass is visible in the commit history rather than pasted in one go.

## Tools I used

- A plain text editor for drafting.
- Git to keep the edits visible as separate commits.
- The course checker (`check.py`) to catch mechanical problems — headings, references, word count.

## What I kept and what I cut

- **Kept** the lamp observation (two lamps, one remembered). It carries the central claim better than any definition would.
- **Kept** "code as a material" — loops, conditionals, randomness — because it ties my old craft to the new one.
- **Cut** roughly 200 words about specific manufacturing techniques. It was interesting but did not move the argument.
- **Rejected** a section on gamification mechanics (points, badges, leaderboards). It would have read like a feature list instead of a design argument.

## What I would do differently

The essay leans on my own observations more than on published work. Next time I would read two or three design texts first and let them argue with each other, instead of adding references at the end.
